Case Study #5260

IBM Network Intelligence

IBM Network Intelligence is an AI product for network operations. An agent works network problems on its own: it correlates alarms and metrics, forms and revises conclusions, and proposes fixes, sometimes running them. A person oversees it. My job was to design how that oversight works, for an agent that keeps working whether or not anyone is watching.

I’ve worked on Network Intelligence for more than two years. Once again, that meant learning a complicated domain well enough to design for it: network operations, where engineers keep carrier and enterprise networks running. They watch routing between networks, streams of telemetry and floods of alarms from devices made by many vendors, and trace each failure back to its cause. All of that while keeping up with AI and agentic systems, which change almost every week.

Challenges to solve:
  • Design for an agent that runs continuously, not a chatbot bolted onto a dashboard
  • Show what needs a person right now, and what changed while they were away
  • Earn trust in the agent’s conclusions before asking anyone to let it act
  • Bring in the tools network teams already use without rebuilding them
My role:
  • Principal product designer, guiding the product from proof of concept to general release and beyond
  • Define the core experience, reasoning patterns and interaction model
  • Develop new patterns for AI experiences, adopted by IBM’s Carbon Design System
  • Work with a global team of developers and engineers
  • Lead research with network operators, and gather feedback from SREs and users to iterate on and improve the product
  • Build working, high-fidelity prototypes in code with Carbon, IBM’s design system

Three questions

Network operations teams don’t lack data. They have alarms, metrics and dashboards spread across several tools, and the correlation lives in someone’s head. Network Intelligence takes on that work: the agent opens an investigation, gathers evidence, names a likely cause and builds a remediation. The investigation is the unit of work, and it moves through stages from Pending to Resolved, sometimes without a person touching it.

So the interface isn’t where the work happens. It’s where a person catches up with work that’s already underway. I designed everything around three questions for the person who just sat down: What needs me right now? What changed while I was away? Can I trust what it concluded?

Every surface says something new

An early home page listed what needed attention, and the first question in review was: isn’t that just repeating the sidebar? It was. That became the rule for the whole layout: every surface has to say something the others can’t. The left rail owns current state, grouped by what each investigation needs from you: a tool approval, a change request, an answer to a question. The center owns transitions: a conclusion that changed, a grade that dropped, an investigation that closed itself. On a quiet night the feed is almost empty, and that’s information, not a failure to fill the space.

People could make home their own, but only by pinning things already on screen, never by starting from a blank page. We’d tried a home page built around a prompt box, and it didn’t work. People don’t arrive with a prompt in mind. They arrive wanting to know what happened.

Showing its work

Every investigation has a Summary, its Observations and its Reasoning: every step the agent took and the evidence behind it. When the agent wants to act, the step says what kind of action it is (read-only, reversible, or rolled back automatically after ten minutes) before anyone approves and runs it. Teams choose which tools the agent always asks about and which it can run on its own, so autonomy grows as trust does.

Other systems can send their own conclusions, too, and I drew a hard line between evidence and testimony. A measurement is evidence. Another agent’s answer is testimony. An investigation that quietly promotes someone else’s conclusion into its own evidence will eventually cite something it can’t defend, so the interface labels those findings as theirs, not ours, and styles them differently so the difference survives a skim.

Patterns for working with AI

Many of the patterns this product needed didn’t exist yet. I designed how multi-agent orchestration shows up in context, so operators can follow what several agents are doing and act on it without digging. I also designed the workflows behind the agent: connecting MCP servers, managing its tools and data, curating the documents it grounds its answers in, and showing where every answer came from.

Several of these patterns were adopted by Carbon, IBM’s design system, so teams across IBM can use them. One of them is a UX pattern I invented for passing network data to the AI as context, so a person can hand it exactly what they want analyzed.

Closing the loop

A fix isn’t done when it’s applied. Findings tracks every change the agent recommended, from needs approval through a 14-day monitoring window, until it’s proven or regresses. It’s also where the agent asks people to confirm what it learned, and where the results add up: changes applied, alerts avoided, hours saved.

From research to direction

A customer discovery workshop surfaced nine problems network teams kept running into: alarm floods, slow root cause, too many tools, growing without adding headcount, blame between domains, trust in AI, stale runbooks, slow onboarding and integration. I sketched responses to each one directly on the working prototype, and reviewed them with product management to decide what to pursue now and what to hold for later. Among them: a handoff summary written for whoever reads it, from an engineer to an executive; autonomy set per workflow and device group instead of per tool; and findings that catch a runbook drifting out of date.

How I worked

I designed in code. Instead of static screens, I built working prototypes on Carbon with realistic data, so the team could click through an investigation, approve a step and watch the queue change. In the last six months alone that came to more than 200 iterations across a dozen prototypes, from onboarding and agent behavior to planning and a digital twin for testing changes before they reach the network.

Each prototype came with an illustrated document covering how it behaves, why, and what changed, so engineering had the reasoning along with the screens. I kept a record of ideas we tried and reversed, too: a chat rail that fought the layout for space, prompt tiles on home, a conversation rendered under the home page. They were tempting ideas that didn’t survive contact, and knowing why is as useful as the design that replaced them.
