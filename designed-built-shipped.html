Case Study #8828

Designed, Built, Shipped

This year I started building my own software. Not prototypes: real apps that I designed, built and shipped to the App Store myself, with AI as my engineering partner. Each one started with a problem from my own life or work, and each one took me further outside what I already knew how to do.

Challenges to solve:
  • Learn to build and ship real software as a designer, with AI as my engineering partner
  • Solve problems I actually have, in my life and my work
  • Get each app through App Store review, from the listing to the privacy policy
  • Keep every app simple and private: no accounts and no servers to run, with iCloud to sync data across devices
My role:
  • Product manager for these apps: deciding what each one should do, what to cut, and when it was ready to ship
  • Product and interface design
  • Built them with Claude Code and Codex: SwiftUI for iPhone, iPad and Mac
  • Testing, accessibility and the App Store releases

BuySellHold

An iPhone stock watchlist built around your own prices.

For years I wanted to track a stock from the moment I started watching it: not what it did over the past month or year, but how it moved once I decided to pay attention. I’d forget what it cost when it caught my eye, and I had no record of the price I’d act on. I’d jot it down or make a mental note, but I always lost the context. In BuySellHold every stock starts with a hold price, plus a low to buy at and a high to sell at. When a price crosses one, the card turns green to buy or purple to sell; otherwise the list stays quiet. It’s like starting my own personal coverage of a stock.

Prices come from Finnhub, using each person’s own free key, kept in the iPhone’s Keychain and sent straight to Finnhub, so there’s no central server. Early versions had an AI assistant built in. It cost money for every question and added a lot of machinery, so I cut it. Now tapping a ticker opens Google’s AI Mode with a prompt already written to start the conversation: “What is the current Wall Street analyst consensus rating (buy, hold, or sell) for Apple (AAPL)?…”

Artifactr

A private library for prompts, their versions and the best things AI makes.

It started with prompts. I’d write one, test it, tweak it, then try it again in a different AI tool, and I couldn’t keep track of which version did what. The same went for the AI skills I was developing: I needed a better way to organize them and compare their output. Artifactr gave each prompt a home. It’s especially good for image generation: I can iterate on a prompt, compare the results, and keep examples of the output right alongside the prompt that made them.

Then I found a second need: keeping the artifacts themselves. When AI gave me a recipe that turned out great, I saved it, and over time that became a collection. Save text and images with categories and tags, search and filter, and copy or share in a tap.

The more I used it, the more it grew. Iterating on prompts showed me I needed version history, so I added it: every change is saved, and I can go back to an earlier version in a tap. It syncs privately through iCloud, so the same library is on my iPhone, iPad and Mac, and it exports and imports backups so nothing is locked in. I use it every day.

Flatbed

One place to capture every request that comes my way at work.

As a principal designer working with teams around the world, I get requests from everywhere: a Slack message, a Jira ticket, an Aha idea, or someone asking in a meeting. Not every job has a ticket behind it, and the ones that do are scattered across different tools. Despite all those tools, I found myself keeping everything in Apple Notes, a way to organize the organizing tools. It was dead simple, but it wasn’t built for this, and at some point it got very hard to keep up with.

Flatbed keeps that simplicity, tailored to my workflow. I can jot down a request the moment I get it, add the links that go with it, and move it through four stages that match how I work: Exploring, Working, Reviewing and Done. Paste a link and it recognizes Figma, Jira and Aha. I can sort requests by when they came in or when I last touched them.

Using it every day is what shaped it. So many requests come with a screenshot or visual reference that I added drag and drop: pull an image straight onto a request and it’s attached. It’s built in SwiftUI for Mac, iPhone and iPad, with iCloud sync, so a request I capture on my phone in a meeting is waiting on my Mac when I’m back at my desk. And I’ve kept it simple on purpose. Productivity apps tend to grow complicated and bloated, and I didn’t want Flatbed to become one more.

How I worked

Every app started with a sketch, by hand or in Figma. From there I worked with Claude Code as my engineering partner: I described what I wanted, it wrote the code, and I reviewed and tested what it built. Sketches worked well, and screenshots marked up with notes worked even better. I learned to phrase requests so they left less room for guessing, and my years of design work, and knowing CSS, helped me say exactly what I meant. I learned a lot of the code itself by watching it work and reading its reasoning.

I tried every AI coding tool I could get my hands on: Replit, Cursor, Windsurf, Antigravity and Devin. I kept coming back to Claude Code and Codex.

These were my first projects in Xcode, so I learned its built-in tools as I went. I tested everything myself and used Claude Code and Codex to review contrast, accessibility and UX best practices: the same checks I run on any product, now with AI as a second reviewer. Artifactr, for example, supports dark mode, with contrast checked throughout. I got feedback from other people along the way, too.

At first it was frustrating: simple changes could take several tries. That got better fast. I got clearer about what I asked for, the tools kept improving, and by the third app we worked well together.

What building changed

These projects changed my day job too. I took what I learned here and applied it to my product design work. I used to spend hours, sometimes days, building complex prototypes in Figma. I still make Figma files as the source of truth, but now I also build highly interactive demos that show how a feature works at a high level of fidelity. Instead of describing to engineers how something should work, I show them.

I’ve also learned to write documentation with code as the artifact, something engineers can work straight into production. I’m not just the designer handing off a file anymore. I’m a collaborator in the code.

Trying all those tools also gave me hands-on experience with agentic systems, which change almost every week. I brought that knowledge straight into my day job, where I work on an agentic NetOps platform.

And AI made it possible to take ideas I’d carried for years and finish them, on my own.
