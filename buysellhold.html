Case Study #8828

BuySellHold

BuySellHold is an iPhone app I designed, built and shipped to the App Store on my own, with AI as my engineering partner. The idea came from a need I’d had for years: keeping track of a stock from the moment I started watching it. I’d notice a stock, forget what it cost when it caught my eye, and have no record of the price I’d act on. This project was my way to finally build that tool, and to learn what it takes for a designer to make real software.

Challenges to solve:
  • Learn to build and ship a native iOS app as a designer, with AI as my engineering partner
  • Remember the price a stock was at when you started watching it
  • Set your own buy and sell prices and see at a glance when a stock crosses one
  • Keep it simple: no account, no ads, your own quote source
My role:
  • Product and interface design, flows and the visual identity
  • Built the iOS app in SwiftUI with Claude Code
  • Accessibility, testing and the App Store release: listing, privacy and support pages, and the preview video

▲ The App Store preview: add a stock, set a range, and watch the card react when the price crosses it.

A designer building the real thing

I’d carried this idea for years. What changed was the tools. I designed each screen, then built the app in SwiftUI with Claude Code: describing what I wanted, reading what it wrote, running it on my phone and pushing back when something didn’t feel right. Along the way I learned how an iOS app is put together, from storing the watchlist on the device and fetching live quotes to keeping a key safe in the Keychain, designing the first-run experience and getting through App Store review.

Working this way changed how I design. I could try an idea in the real app in minutes instead of describing it in a mockup, so decisions came from using the thing, not from guessing.

Your price, not the market’s

Every stock starts with a hold price: what it cost when you decided to watch it. From there you set a low price to buy at and a high price to sell at, either as a price or as a percentage away from hold. That one record answers the question I could never answer before: what was it when I started paying attention, and where did I say I’d act?

Buy low, sell high, at a glance

When a price drops below your low, the card turns green: that’s your buy signal. When it climbs past your high, it turns purple: time to sell. Everything else stays neutral, so the watchlist only asks for attention when one of your own prices is crossed. Sorting by change from hold puts the movers on top.

Live prices, your own key

Prices come from Finnhub, a market data service. Instead of running a server and paying for a data feed, the app has each person connect their own free Finnhub account during setup; signup takes about a minute, and the app links straight to it. The key is checked with a real quote before it’s saved, then kept in the iPhone’s Keychain. Requests go directly from the phone to Finnhub, so there’s no account to create with me and no server in the middle. Pull down or tap refresh and every stock on the list updates at once, along with each company’s name.

Cutting the AI assistant

The first versions had an AI assistant built in: ask about a stock and it would search the web and summarize what analysts were saying. It worked, but it meant paying for every question and carrying a lot of extra machinery for one feature. So I took it out. Now tapping a stock’s ticker opens Google’s AI Mode with the question already written: the analyst consensus, the average price target and the reasons behind it. People still get the research in one tap, and the app stays simple and free to run.

Shipping it

Getting BuySellHold onto the App Store was the challenge I set myself, and it’s there now: listing, privacy and support pages, an accessibility pass on type and contrast, and the preview video above. It’s a small app, but every part of it, from the first sketch to the release, is mine.
