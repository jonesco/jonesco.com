Case Study #8828

Shipped

This year I started building my own software. Not prototypes: real apps that I designed, built and shipped to the App Store myself, with AI as my engineering partner. Each one started with a problem from my own life or work, and each one took me further outside what I already knew how to do.

Challenges to solve:
  • Learn to build and ship real software as a designer, with AI as my engineering partner
  • Solve problems I actually have, in my life and my work
  • Get each app through App Store review, from the listing to the privacy policy
  • Keep every app simple and private: no accounts, no servers to run
My role:
  • Product and interface design for all three apps
  • Built them with Claude Code: SwiftUI for iPhone, iPad and Mac, and Electron for Flatbed’s first version
  • Testing, accessibility and the App Store releases

App 1 of 3

BuySellHold

An iPhone stock watchlist built around your own prices.

For years I wanted to track a stock from the moment I started watching it. I’d forget what it cost when it caught my eye, and I had no record of the price I’d act on. In BuySellHold every stock starts with a hold price, plus a low to buy at and a high to sell at. When a price crosses one, the card turns green to buy or purple to sell; otherwise the list stays quiet.

Prices come from Finnhub, using each person’s own free key, kept in the iPhone’s Keychain and sent straight to Finnhub. Early versions had an AI assistant built in. It cost money for every question and added a lot of machinery, so I cut it: tapping a ticker now opens Google’s AI Mode with the question already written.

What it taught me: My first app taught me how an iOS app is put together: storing data on the device, calling a live market data service, keeping a key secure, and getting through App Store review for the first time.

App 2 of 3

Artifactr

A private library for prompts, their versions and the best things AI makes.

It started with prompts. I’d write one, test it, tweak it, then try it again in a different AI tool, and I couldn’t keep track of which version did what. Artifactr gave each prompt a home and a version history, so I could iterate without losing the thread. Then I found a second use: keeping the artifacts themselves. When AI gave me a recipe that turned out great, I saved it, and over time that became a collection. Save text and images with categories and tags, search and filter, and copy or share in a tap.

It syncs privately through iCloud, so the same library is on my iPhone, iPad and Mac, and it exports and imports backups so nothing is locked in.

What it taught me: Artifactr was my first app across iPhone, iPad and Mac, and my first with iCloud sync: one design adapting to three screen sizes, with data that follows you from one to the next. It was also the first one I shipped.

App 3 of 3

Flatbed

One place to capture every request that comes my way at work.

Requests come at me from everywhere: a Slack message, a Jira ticket, an Aha idea, or someone asking in a meeting. Not every request has a ticket behind it, and the ones that did were scattered across different tools. So I started keeping everything in something dead simple: Apple Notes. But Notes isn’t built for this, and at some point it got very hard to keep up with. So I asked myself: could I build something with the simplicity of Apple Notes, tailored to my workflow? Flatbed is that app. I can jot down a request the moment I get it, add the links and images that go with it, and move it through four stages: Exploring, Working, Reviewing and Done. Paste a link and it recognizes Figma, Jira and Aha. The layout borrows from Mail and Notes so it feels at home on a Mac.

I built it twice. An Electron version came first because it was the fastest way to a working app and to prove the design. Then I rebuilt it natively in SwiftUI for Mac, iPhone and iPad, with iCloud sync, keeping both versions matched as the design changed.

What it taught me: Flatbed taught me to prototype fast and then rebuild properly: try the idea with the quickest tools, then commit to the native version once the design holds up.

What building changed

Building these apps changed how I design. I can try an idea in the real app in minutes instead of describing it in a mockup, so decisions come from using the thing, not from guessing. I understand the constraints engineers work with because I’ve run into them myself. And AI made it possible to take ideas I’d carried for years and finish them, on my own.
